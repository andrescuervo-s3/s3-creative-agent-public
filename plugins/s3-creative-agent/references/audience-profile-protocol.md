# Audience Profile Research Protocol (Studio 3 Marketing)

Purpose: Develop accurate, evidence-based audience profiles grounded in third-party, independent research, using only credible demographic, behavioral, and category-relevant sources -- not self-published brand content or marketing firms.

---

## Source Quality Framework

All audience research must follow this hierarchy. Higher tiers override lower tiers when facts conflict.

**Tier 1 — Authoritative (highest weight)**
Government data (U.S. Census, BLS, CDC, NIH, FTC, state agencies), peer-reviewed academic studies, professional licensing boards (state bar associations, medical boards, NCCRS), and established research institutions (Pew Research, Brookings, Urban Institute).

**Tier 2 — Verified Independent**
Investigative journalism with cited sources, neutral review platforms with verified reviews (Healthgrades, Avvo, BBB), professional association reports (ABA, AMA, NAHB, SHRM), and industry trade surveys with disclosed methodology.

**Tier 3 — Observed (positioning only, never proof)**
The client's own website, social channels, and marketing materials. Use to understand how the client presents themselves, not as evidence of audience behavior.

**Never use:** Marketing agency blogs, vendor whitepapers, AI-generated listicles, SEO-first articles with unclear methodology, or any source that sells services to the audience it describes.

---

## Research Order

Start with what you have, then go deeper:

1. **Client data first** — Google Analytics (if connected): actual site visitors, demographics, geography, device, top landing pages, referral sources. SEO keyword data from Step 0. This is real behavioral data about the client's current audience.
2. **Location-specific authoritative data** — Census/ACS data for the client's service area: income, household composition, age distribution, education, housing. State-level licensing boards or professional registries for regulated industries.
3. **Category-specific research** — Pew, academic studies, professional association surveys that describe how this audience type makes decisions, what they fear, what trust signals matter, and what drives action.
4. **General web research** — only to fill remaining gaps, and only from Tier 1 or Tier 2 sources.

---

## Step 1: Define Audience Categories

Identify up to three core audience groups based on the client's offering and context. Use intake documents or discovery interviews to understand:

- Who is the client trying to reach?
- Who currently buys, uses, or interacts with their product or service?
- Who influences or refers these buyers?

### Examples Across Sectors

**Healthcare and Aesthetics**
- Chronic care patients (e.g., diabetes, cardiovascular, autoimmune)
- Elective procedure seekers (e.g., LASIK, joint replacement, cosmetic surgery)
- Medspa clientele (e.g., patients seeking injectables, facials, skin tightening, wellness infusions)
- Plastic surgery candidates (e.g., patients pursuing body contouring, rhinoplasty, reconstruction)
- Referring providers (e.g., primary care, dermatologists, OB/GYNs referring to aesthetics)

**Legal**
- Divorce and custody clients
- Criminal defense clients
- Mass tort or class-action plaintiffs
- Attorney referral sources (e.g., boutique firms, solo practitioners)

**Home Services**
- Emergency homeowners (e.g., burst pipes, storm damage, infestations)
- High-value renovators (e.g., luxury kitchen/bath remodel clients)
- Real estate professionals (e.g., agents, inspectors referring service vendors)

**B2B Tech and Professional Services**
- Mid-market CFOs managing SaaS procurement
- IT decision-makers for infrastructure and security contracts
- Implementation partners (e.g., agencies, MSPs, dev shops collaborating with software vendors)
- Corporate legal, HR, or compliance buyers for professional services or platforms

---

## Step 2: Frame Core Research Questions

For each audience category, answer the following:
- Who are they demographically? (Age, income, household type, professional role, geography)
- What are their primary needs or goals?
- What pain points, risks, or constraints affect their behavior?
- What behaviors or search patterns define how they evaluate vendors or solutions?
- What social proof, certifications, or trust signals do they seek?

---

## Step 3: Perform Third-Party Research Using Trusted Sources

### Acceptable Source Types
- Government agencies: (e.g., CDC, BJS, FTC, SBA, U.S. Census, NIH, Department of Education)
- Research institutes / think tanks: (e.g., Pew Research, Brookings, Urban Institute)
- Professional associations: (e.g., ABA, AMA, AIA, IEEE, SHRM, NAHB)
- Neutral review platforms and directories: (e.g., BBB, Healthgrades, Avvo, Super Lawyers, Houzz, G2, Capterra)
- Academic publications or journals with population or behavioral data
- Statistical reports or sector surveys from neutral nonprofits or trade groups

### Do Not Use
- Agency blogs or marketing firms
- Corporate thought leadership or whitepapers
- Self-reported brand case studies
- AI-generated listicles, SEO-first articles, or blogs with unclear methodology

---

## Step 4: Extract Behavioral and Demographic Indicators

From each valid source:
- Pull quantitative data where possible (percentages, trends, counts)
- Extract qualitative insights that explain mindset, motivation, or emotional context
- Look for behavioral triggers (what leads them to seek help, buy, switch, refer, etc.)
- Prioritize multi-source corroboration (don't anchor on a single fact)

---

## Step 5: Structure the Audience Profile

For each audience segment, write a profile using this structure:

1. **Demographics**: Geography, life stage, professional role, urgency context, and relevant qualifiers
2. **Mindset**: What they are protecting, seeking, fearing, or motivated by
3. **Attitude**: How they evaluate options, what they demand, and what they reject
4. **Perception**: What must be true for trust to form; how they identify authority or safety
5. **Evidence**: Include 2-3 plain-text source URLs to support claims -- focus on credibility, not volume

---

## Citation Standards
- Include source name + full plain-text URL for each cited data point
- Do not use inline hyperlinks, footnotes, or redirect links
- Ensure URLs point directly to the research page (not blog summaries or PDF downloads)
- Always confirm the link is active, reputable, and independent

---

## Example Use Cases
- Inform creative strategy with real buyer psychology
- Validate personas for pitch decks or proposals
- Ground messaging direction in documented audience reality
- Support media targeting and keyword decisions with factual context
